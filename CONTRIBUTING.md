# Contributing to Means+Measures

**Equity of means. Equality of measures.**

Means+Measures welcomes contributions that improve the accessibility, reliability, transparency, and usefulness of the systems we build.

Participation should not depend on professional credentials, institutional affiliation, or familiarity with the systems being examined. Contributions are evaluated on their substance, evidence, and impact.

## Ways to contribute

Contributions extend beyond writing code. They may include:

- **Code and infrastructure:** Implementing features, resolving defects, improving performance, and strengthening system reliability.
- **Documentation:** Clarifying technical concepts, correcting inaccuracies, and making information easier to understand and use.
- **Accessibility:** Identifying barriers to participation and proposing or implementing improvements.
- **Data and research:** Examining data quality, documenting methodology, validating findings, and identifying unsupported conclusions.
- **Testing and review:** Reproducing defects, evaluating changes, identifying unintended consequences, and providing constructive technical criticism.
- **Design and usability:** Improving how people interact with systems, particularly when existing interfaces assume specialized knowledge or institutional access.

Contributions grounded in direct experience are welcome alongside those informed by formal technical or academic expertise.

## How to contribute

You do not need to write code to participate. Choose the path that fits what you want to contribute.

### Path 1: Report a problem or propose an improvement

1. Open the relevant repository's **Issues** tab.
2. Check existing issues to see whether the matter has already been raised.
3. Select **New issue** and choose an available template, or open a blank issue.
4. Describe what you observed, what you expected, and why the difference matters.
5. Include relevant context, reproduction steps, evidence, or supporting documentation when available.

You can also comment on existing issues to provide additional evidence, observations, or proposed solutions.

For open-ended questions or proposals that would benefit from discussion before implementation, use the repository's **Discussions** tab when enabled.

### Path 2: Submit documentation or code changes

1. **Find the repository.** Identify the project you want to improve and review its README, documentation, open issues, and recent pull requests.
2. **Fork or branch.** Fork the repository to your account, or create a branch directly if you have the necessary permissions.
3. **Make your changes.** Keep the work focused on a clear objective, following the repository's existing conventions.
4. **Validate.** Run relevant tests, checks, or documentation previews. Record anything that could not be verified.
5. **Open a pull request.** Target the repository's default branch unless its documentation specifies otherwise. Describe the change, its purpose, related issues, and validation results.
6. **Participate in review.** Respond to questions, discuss proposed revisions, and update the branch as needed.
7. **Resolution.** Maintainers review the contribution and may merge it, request changes, defer it, or decline it with an explanation where practical.

For a small documentation correction, GitHub's built-in file editor can provide a direct route to proposing a change without configuring a local development environment.

### Path 3: Contribute evidence, experience, or feedback

If you have encountered a barrier, identified an unsupported claim, or observed a system behaving differently from its documented purpose, that information can itself be a meaningful contribution.

Open an issue or join a relevant discussion. Describe the observation and provide whatever supporting detail is appropriate.

You are not expected to supply a complete technical solution before identifying a problem.

### Security vulnerabilities

Do not disclose exploitable vulnerabilities in public issues or discussions. Follow the affected repository's `SECURITY.md` or its private vulnerability reporting mechanism, where available.

## Before proposing substantial changes

Review existing documentation, open issues, and project decisions to understand the current scope and rationale.

For changes involving significant architectural decisions, new dependencies, or broader project direction, opening an issue before implementation can help establish context and avoid conflicting work.

Small corrections and clearly scoped fixes can generally proceed directly to a pull request.

## Standards for contributions

### Accessibility is part of functionality

Accessibility is a design consideration from the beginning, not a corrective layer added after implementation.

Changes should account for the people expected to use the system, including those navigating without specialized knowledge, assistive technology, or privileged institutional access.

### Claims require appropriate evidence

Distinguish observed facts, documented requirements, analytical conclusions, and proposals.

Where a change relies on factual assertions, technical measurements, or external information, provide enough context for others to evaluate those claims.

Do not present assumptions as established requirements.

### Evaluate outcomes, not standing

The merits of a contribution do not depend on the contributor's title, employer, academic credentials, or organizational influence.

Evaluate changes according to their accuracy, functionality, maintainability, accessibility, and demonstrated consequences.

### Preserve context and intent

Understand why an existing implementation or decision was made before replacing it.

Distinguish documented requirements from implementation choices. Avoid introducing new constraints, dependencies, or complexity without a demonstrated reason.

### Make disagreement productive

Questions, challenges, and corrections are part of accountable development.

Critique the reasoning, implementation, evidence, and outcomes of a proposal. Substantive disagreement is not, by itself, a failure of collaboration.

Review should make decisions more defensible, not merely more agreeable.

## Review and acceptance

Submitting a contribution does not guarantee its acceptance.

Maintainers evaluate proposed changes against the repository's purpose, established requirements, technical merits, and effects on the people and systems involved.

Review may result in acceptance, requested revision, deferral, or rejection. Where practical, consequential decisions should include an explanation sufficient for contributors to understand the reasoning.

A technically functional change may still warrant revision when it creates unnecessary barriers, obscures provenance, or undermines the project's established objectives.

## Documentation and attribution

Contributions should preserve relevant attribution, licensing information, and provenance.

Do not submit materials without the rights or authorization necessary to contribute them.

When using generated code, analysis, or documentation, contributors remain responsible for reviewing its accuracy, compatibility, and applicable licensing obligations.

## Our commitment

Institutions bear responsibility **down through systems** for making the means of participation accessible.

Authority rises **up through people** who question, evaluate, and hold those institutions accountable.

We apply those principles to our own work as well as to the systems we build.

Participation creates the opportunity to contribute. Evidence, implementation, and impact establish the merits of the contribution.
