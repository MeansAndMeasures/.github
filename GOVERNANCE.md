# Governance at Means+Measures

**Equity of means. Equality of measures.**

Governance determines how authority is exercised, how decisions are evaluated, and how those affected by an institution can question its actions.

At Means+Measures, governance begins with two corresponding obligations:

- **Institutions bear responsibility down through systems** for making the means of participation accessible.
- **Authority rises up through people** who question, evaluate, and hold those institutions accountable.

Neither participation nor accountability should depend on privilege, institutional standing, or proximity to those making decisions.

## 1. Purpose and scope

This document establishes the principles and expectations governing collaboration, technical decisions, and accountability across Means+Measures repositories.

It describes how decisions should be justified, challenged, documented, and evaluated. It does not, by itself, establish corporate governance arrangements, membership rights, or legal authority.

Individual repositories may document additional decision-making procedures appropriate to their projects. Such procedures should remain consistent with the principles established here.

## 2. Equity of means

**Lack of privilege is not lack of access.**

The opportunity to participate should not be conditioned on professional credentials, organizational affiliation, financial resources, or familiarity with institutional processes.

Meaningful participation requires more than an open invitation. It requires accessible information, understandable procedures, and practical ways to contribute, question, and challenge decisions.

Responsibility for those conditions belongs to the systems and institutions exercising authority, not solely to the people seeking access.

## 3. Equality of measures

**Impacts are judged on their own merits, not gifted those of the institution.**

Decisions, proposals, contributions, and institutional actions should be evaluated against consistent, relevant, and defensible standards.

Institutional reputation, seniority, expertise, or organizational size may provide context. None substitutes for evidence of correctness, effectiveness, or impact.

The same principle applies to Means+Measures itself. Our work and decisions remain subject to the standards we apply elsewhere.

## 4. Decision-making authority

Authority carries responsibility for the decisions made under it.

Within individual repositories, maintainers exercise the technical and administrative permissions assigned to them. Those permissions enable project stewardship; they do not establish that a maintainer's reasoning is beyond review.

Decision-making should distinguish:

- **Requirements:** Established obligations, constraints, and accepted project objectives.
- **Evidence:** Verifiable information relevant to the decision.
- **Judgment:** Interpretations and choices made where evidence or requirements do not determine a single outcome.
- **Implementation:** The selected means of satisfying a requirement or achieving an objective.

An existing implementation is not automatically a requirement. A maintainer's preference is not automatically a project standard.

Where multiple approaches can satisfy the same objective, decisions should consider their relative merits rather than treating familiarity or incumbency as sufficient justification.

## 5. Participation and representation

People affected by a system may possess knowledge unavailable to those who designed or maintain it.

Direct experience, technical expertise, research, and institutional knowledge provide different kinds of evidence. Their relevance depends on the question being examined.

Participation should support:

- Raising issues and proposing changes.
- Providing evidence, observations, and corrections.
- Questioning requirements, assumptions, and methods.
- Reviewing proposed decisions and their consequences.
- Challenging outcomes through documented, constructive processes.

Participation does not imply that every proposal must be adopted or that every disagreement must be resolved by consensus.

It does require that contributions be considered on their substance rather than dismissed because of the contributor's standing.

## 6. Transparency and provenance

Accountability depends on the ability to understand how a decision was reached.

Consequential project decisions should preserve sufficient context to identify:

- What was decided and what problem the decision addressed.
- Which requirements and evidence informed it.
- What alternatives or tradeoffs were materially considered.
- Who exercised the relevant decision-making authority.
- What remains uncertain, unresolved, or subject to reconsideration.

Documentation should distinguish evidence from inference and proposals from approved decisions.

Summaries may simplify presentation, but should not erase qualifications, provenance, or relationships necessary to understand the underlying reasoning.

Transparency is not measured by the volume of documentation. It is measured by whether the relevant decision can be meaningfully examined.

## 7. Review, disagreement, and challenge

Disagreement is a legitimate part of accountable governance.

A challenge to a decision should be evaluated according to its reasoning, evidence, and relevance. It should not be dismissed solely because a decision has already been made or because the person questioning it lacks formal authority.

### Raising a concern

For repository-specific concerns, contributors may open an issue, participate in an existing discussion, or comment on a relevant pull request.

A useful challenge identifies the decision or implementation at issue and explains the concern, with supporting context where available.

### Reviewing a concern

Maintainers should examine whether the concern identifies:

- A factual or technical error.
- A conflict with established requirements.
- An accessibility or participation barrier.
- An unsupported assumption or conclusion.
- A consequential impact not previously considered.
- A potentially preferable implementation.

Review should address the substance of the concern, including evidence that may contradict an earlier conclusion.

### Resolving a concern

A review may result in maintaining the existing decision, revising it, requesting additional evidence, or reopening the underlying question.

Where practical, consequential resolutions should document the reasoning behind the outcome.

A decision's finality within a particular workflow does not establish its permanent correctness. New evidence, changed conditions, or identified errors may warrant reconsideration.

## 8. Accountability for outcomes

A documented process is not, by itself, evidence of a successful outcome.

Governance should examine what systems actually do, including whether they achieve their stated purposes and whether their operation creates barriers or consequences inconsistent with those purposes.

Evaluation should distinguish:

- **Intent:** What the system or decision was meant to accomplish.
- **Implementation:** What was actually built or done.
- **Operation:** How the system behaves in practice.
- **Impact:** The consequences for the people and institutions involved.

Good intentions do not negate adverse impacts. Conversely, criticism should not presume misconduct or failure without supporting evidence.

Corrective action should respond to demonstrated problems and their underlying causes, rather than treating documentation or acknowledgment as a substitute for improvement.

## 9. Stewardship and project continuity

Maintainers are responsible for the integrity and continuity of the projects they administer.

That responsibility includes preserving relevant project context, reviewing changes, managing technical dependencies, and ensuring that decisions remain traceable as systems evolve.

Stewardship should not turn undocumented preferences into permanent constraints or make the continuity of a project dependent on knowledge held exclusively by particular individuals.

Where practical, project documentation should make established requirements, responsibilities, and significant technical decisions understandable to future contributors and maintainers.

## 10. Changes to governance

This document is itself subject to scrutiny and revision.

Proposed changes may be submitted through the contribution process and should explain the problem being addressed, the proposed modification, and its implications for participation, authority, or accountability.

Changes affecting established decision-making rights or organizational responsibilities require review by the authority competent to approve them. Repository-level acceptance alone does not establish authority over matters outside that repository.

Governance revisions should preserve the history and reasoning necessary to understand substantive changes.

## Governing principle

**Down through systems. Up through people.**

Institutions are responsible for making participation possible. People must retain the means to examine, question, and hold those institutions accountable.

The legitimacy of authority is not established by the institution's standing alone. It depends on the standards by which that authority is exercised and the consequences by which its actions can be judged.
